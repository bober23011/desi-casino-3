How the pegboard is set up

The board is a triangle of offset pegs: each lower row is one peg wider than the row above. When a ball hits a peg, it has a fair chance of moving left or right, then repeats that split at every row it crosses. After the final row, it drops into one of the bottom pockets — always one more pocket than the number of rows.

Because every route is a chain of fifty-fifty turns, most balls collect near the middle and only a few drift all the way to the edges. Mathematicians call this a Galton board, and the result is a binomial curve. In casino form, that curve is turned into a payout table: common center pockets pay less, while rare edge pockets pay more.

Multipliers, probability, and the house edge

Each pocket’s multiplier is tied to its probability, then trimmed slightly by the house edge. If you multiply the chance of each pocket by its payout, the total ends up just below one; that difference is the operator’s margin. In many published versions, the return to player sits somewhere around 97% to 99%, depending on the studio and selected settings.

Important point: no row count or risk level can turn a negative edge into a positive one over the long run. The settings change volatility — how bumpy the results feel — not the underlying math.

Licensed platforms usually run the board with a certified random number generator, and provably fair versions publish seed data so the result can be checked later. What no legitimate version can do is help you steer the ball.

Six useful terms

Pocket
The slot at the bottom of the board that sets the final multiplier for a ball.
Rows
The number of peg lines the ball crosses; more rows create more pockets and sharper odds.
Risk level
A preset that stretches or compresses the payout table without changing the long-run return much.
House margin
The small difference between true odds and paid odds that supports the operator.
Provably fair
A system of published hashes that lets players verify the result after the round ends.
Variance
The size of the swings around the average result, which is what the risk setting really affects.

Straight answers to five common questions

Is every drop actually random?

On regulated sites, yes. Each turn is produced by an audited random number generator, and provably fair versions let you confirm later that the result was locked in before the ball moved.

Do more rows mean better payouts?

No. Extra rows add pockets and stretch the odds curve, so edge multipliers grow while their chance of landing gets smaller. The overall return remains about the same.

Which risk level is the best?

There is no mathematically best choice. Low risk smooths the ride, while high risk concentrates value in rare outer hits. Choose based on your budget and tolerance for swings.

Can I test the game without paying?

Yes. The board on this page uses virtual credits, and many platforms in India also have free play versions with the same odds. The math stays the same when real money is involved.

Does timing the button change the result?

No. The landing spot is set by the random number system, not by when you press drop. Any pattern that seems real is usually coincidence.
